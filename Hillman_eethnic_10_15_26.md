AMMON HILLMAN AND THE BABYLONIAN TIME RESEARCH
Ammon Hillman and the Eethnic and Babylonian Time Research
A comparative investigation of ancient texts and institutional authority
Prepared for Andrew Dube • 14 October 2026
Main findings
Hillman has a documented doctorate in classics and a verifiable publication history in ancient pharmacology. That qualifies him as a trained classicist; it does not make each of his reconstructions reliable. The strongest connections to your documents concern the ownership of specialist knowledge, the use of prestigious ancient names to authorize texts, and disputes over who may interpret religious experience. These connections can be demonstrated without accepting his broad claims about drugs or religious origins. [1][2][3][4]
Your BT-01 through BT-11 ledger already makes the crucial historical distinction. Mesopotamian scholarship, Judean contact with Babylonian institutions, and transmission of astronomical schemes have substantial support. A specific covert priestly organization moving intact between those settings has not been identified. This report retains that distinction and the original ledger numbering. [U2]
Three discoveries sharpen the comparison. The Hippocratic On the Sacred Disease describes an explanatory maneuver closely resembling the Eethnic shield. PGM XIII physically joins Greek ritual instructions, Mosaic authority, and astral fate in one late antique manuscript. At Sippar, the strongest accessible study supports Judean mercantile integration but explicitly qualifies the proposed connection to priestly families. [10][14][15][20]
Two corrections matter especially. “Pharisee” cannot simply be translated as “Persianizer.” And the Babylonian origin of Zurvanite theology is a disputed scholarly reconstruction: an older specialist account supports it, whereas Albert de Jong challenges the evidence for that reconstruction. Neither position establishes a deliberate takeover by a traceable organization. [28][29][30][34][35]

Testing the arguments about drugs and ritual
What the ancient evidence supports
Drug-related language and practices are genuinely present in ancient literature. In Odyssey 4.219–232, Helen puts a pharmakon into wine to relieve grief, and the narrative associates her knowledge with Egypt. This is explicit literary evidence for an imagined mood-altering preparation. The passage does not identify its modern chemical composition or establish how often an audience consumed such preparations. [8][25]
The Hymn to Demeter supplies a useful control case. Lines 205–211 specify water, barley meal, and pennyroyal for Demeter’s drink; the poem also presents restricted rites. An argument for an additional psychoactive ingredient needs further evidence. Secrecy does not fill an ingredient list, and an account of a divine drink is not a laboratory analysis. Conversely, the poem’s silence cannot rule out every preparation used in later historical ceremonies. [9]
Material evidence can sometimes answer a narrower question more firmly. Arie, Rosen, and Namdar’s study of the Judahite shrine at Arad reports cannabinoid residues on one altar and frankincense on another, with analyses conducted in two laboratories. That supports a local ritual use of cannabis. It does not establish a universal ancient sacrament, a specific experience for every participant, or the origins of Christianity. The reported chemistry is evidence; reconstruction of purpose and subjective effects is interpretation. [16]
Where the inference becomes too large
Philippe Charlier’s contemporary BMCR review identifies concrete weaknesses in The Chemical Muse: moving from Virgil’s poppy imagery to claims about the poet’s experience; identifying mythic substances too confidently; and inferring narcotic use by audiences from Helen’s drug in Homer. The review also objects to Hillman’s statement about Soranus and gynecological literature, pointing to the Hippocratic corpus. Its tone is hostile, but these objections are independently testable. Its book page references, including pp. 45, 50, 91–101, and 145, are the reviewer’s references, not evidence of a full-book rereading here. [5]
The methodological issue is the size of the inference. A word for a drug establishes a lexical possibility. A recipe establishes a textual prescription. Residue can establish a material substance at a particular site. None automatically establishes widespread use, political causation, a ritual’s chemical identity, or a continuous administering institution. Each additional conclusion needs its own evidence.
Hillman’s broad emphasis on neglected pharmacological material is therefore worth examining passage by passage. Claims that intoxication generated democracy, or that a common concealed ritual explains later religious institutions, remain much harder to establish. An adequate test would compare mundane medical uses, hostile accusations, fictional scenes, and actual ritual instructions instead of treating them as interchangeable descriptions of one practice. [4]
Ancient arguments that directly illuminate the Eethnic shield
Explanations that protect their own authority
The opening of On the Sacred Disease attacks purifiers and ritual specialists who call an illness divine while disguising their lack of knowledge. The author describes a self-protecting arrangement: recovery benefits the practitioners’ reputation; failure is attributed to the gods. This closely matches your distinction between correcting conduct in response to evidence and defending an explanation that insulates its custodian. [10][U1]
The connection is textual and conceptual. It is not evidence that the Hippocratic writer knew a Babylonian priestly network. The treatise is itself a competitive intervention by a medical author claiming a better explanation, and it should not be converted into neutral proof that every religious practitioner was fraudulent. Your framework becomes more exact when it tests that author’s proposed remedy by the same standard it applies to his rivals.
Written representations can acquire borrowed authority
In Phaedrus 274c–277a, Theuth offers writing as an aid to memory and wisdom; Thamus questions whether written reminders produce understanding. A written statement cannot answer a fresh question in the way a knowledgeable speaker can. The same story attributes other technical arts, including astronomy, to Theuth. It is a particularly close ancient comparison for your concern that an authorized representation can be mistaken for the living process it represents. [11][U2]
Here pharmakon belongs to the discussion of a remedy for memory. Its lexical range cannot turn the argument into a concealed drug recipe. Plato offers a philosophical test of representation, not a documented history of Babylonian institutional transmission. The defensible comparison is how an artifact acquires authority and how that authority remains answerable to questions. [25]
Claims of revelation also regulate access
Your use of Malachi 2:7–9 already treats priestly knowledge as carrying an obligation that priests can violate. That internal critique matters: the Eethnic question need not assume that religion and correction are inherently opposed. The question is whether an institution permits correction of its interpretation and its conduct. [U1]
Hillman is relevant at this level because disputes about translation concern the control of interpretation. But his counterinterpretation must remain corrigible too. A claim that every objection demonstrates suppression would recreate the very structure your document criticizes. The same standard should apply to priest, academic reviewer, dissident translator, and architectural model.
Where the corpora genuinely intersect
Pliny joins medicine religion and astrology
Pliny’s Natural History 30.1ff explicitly connects the prestige of magic with medicine, religion, and astrology. The scholarly introduction in Defining Magic emphasizes both that combination and Pliny’s heterogeneous list of authorities, including Zoroaster, Greek philosophers, Moses, and figures from myth. This is an ancient Latin text joining several of your central categories. It shows how a Roman author organized them polemically; his list is not a membership register of one transhistorical organization. [12]
Diodorus describes Babylonian specialists through Greek categories
Diodorus 2.29–31 describes Chaldean specialists and their astral knowledge. Jones and Steele compare that account with cuneiform evidence. Some statements match Babylonian practices; others blend Egyptian and Babylonian star systems or introduce cosmology unattested in Babylonian texts. Greek testimony thus genuinely intersects Mesopotamian scholarship, but it requires comparison with Akkadian evidence. A skilled Greek reading alone cannot settle whether Diodorus accurately reports a Babylonian practice. [13]
A physical Greek manuscript invokes Moses and astral authority
PGM XIII is an especially useful new object for this research. The Würzburg catalogue identifies it as P.Leid. I 395, now Leiden J 395, a Greek codex dated 301–400 CE. Its contents include versions of the Eighth Book of Moses, initiation and divination instructions, and other spells. This is a recoverable manuscript where a Jewish ancestral authority appears inside a Greek ritual collection. [14]
Matthew Andrews’s 2026 introduction describes a ritual seeking encounter with a supreme god and relief from astral fate. It includes ingestion of images of the astrological hours and claims to sacred, secret knowledge. That combines authority, ritual, embodied action, and time in one textual setting. Ingesting inscribed images does not by itself demonstrate ingestion of an intoxicant. [15]
For your framework, this is stronger than comparing isolated symbols: the conjunction is present in one object. Historically, however, it belongs to late antique Egypt, many centuries after the Judean exile. Attribution to Moses does not establish Mosaic authorship. A connection to earlier Babylonian astronomy would require identifying particular inherited procedures or concepts and their intermediaries.
Babylon and pharmakeia occur in the same biblical passage
Revelation 18:23 combines the condemnation of Babylon’s commercial power with the dative φαρμακείᾳ, pharmakeia, in its account of deceiving the nations. This is a real lexical intersection with Hillman’s field and your Babylon imagery. The term can concern magical or sorcerous practice; the passage does not specify a chemical, dose, priestly office, or historical transmission route. An apocalyptic accusation cannot independently establish a narcotics economy. [17][26]

What the existing Babylonian leads now establish
The Sippar evidence supports contact more precisely than capture
Kaššāya’s marriage agreements, BM 68921 and BM 65149, connect the Judean Arih family to Babylonian mercantile society. Alstola says neither Marduk-rēmanni nor his family occurs in these documents: they shared acquaintances. Identifying a Guzānu in Marduk-rēmanni’s archive with Kaššāya’s groom remains possible, not certain. [20]
Only one marriage witness is identified as holding an Ebabbar prebend, Bān-zēri, a baker. Alstola characterizes the groom’s family as mercantile. This narrows the earlier priestly-marriage suggestion without erasing real temple and commercial connections. These documents show a social environment in which exchange could occur; they do not record an astronomy lesson, conversion, or infiltration plan. [20][U2]
The Borsippa sēpiru and BM 25563 lead, and the eclipse-ritual testimony discussed later in your document, remain useful targets. They have not been independently recollated here. An alphabetic scribe’s administrative appearance should not silently become proof of training in cuneiform astronomy. The next evidential step is to establish what each named person actually did in the cited text. [U2]
A numerical correspondence gives BT 08 real specificity
Drawnel’s 2025 Time Intervals article reconstructs a relationship between the eighteen-part day and night in 1 Enoch 72 and the 360-shekel water-clock scheme of Enūma Anu Enlil XIV. His earlier study also compares schematic clauses with BM 17175+17284 and MUL.APIN. These are identifiable comparanda, rather than a resemblance between sacred numbers. [22][23]

For your architecture, the strongest research question is who controlled those adjustments and benefited from them. A rule that changes to accommodate observation behaves differently from a rule that denies observations to preserve itself. Standardization can support both technical competence and unequal control; proving one does not automatically prove the other.

Corrections and qualifications for the Babylonian Patch
Zurvanism has a real scholarly debate behind it
Gnoli’s Iranica article supports a model in which contact with Babylonian astral culture helped transform Iranian dualism and encouraged fatalism. That gives part of the Patch a serious scholarly antecedent. It would be inaccurate to dismiss every proposed Babylonian connection as invented. Gnoli nevertheless offers a historical interpretation, not documentary identification of the agents and intentions alleged in the Patch. [35][U3]
De Jong questions a distinct Zurvanite movement and the proposed Babylonian or Greek origins. Yet he argues that the myth represented fifth-century Sasanian court theology, citing Ełišē’s account of Mihr-Narseh’s letter urging Armenians to accept Zoroastrianism. This is a specific reported use of theology for imperial purposes. It supports a bounded political connection; it does not establish that Zurvanism became Persia’s state religion immediately after Babylon’s conquest. [34]
The Gathic twins and later Zurvan myth are separate evidential steps
Yasna 30.3–4 speaks of opposed twin spirits, with the interpretation and relationship to Ahura Mazdā debated. Gnoli distinguishes that problem from the later formulation in which Zurvan fathers Ohrmazd and Ahriman. The Patch should not substitute the later genealogy directly into the Gathic passage or present Ahura Mazdā and Angra Mainyu as an uncontroversial original pair of brothers. A claim about religious transformation requires showing the intervening interpretations. [35]
Loss of evidence does not establish an organized erasure
De Jong discusses the possibility that late Sasanian theological consolidation helped marginalize Zurvanite formulations. That differs from a demonstrated systematic purge after the Islamic conquest. The Patch’s erasure claim requires manuscript history, explicit condemnations, or institutional evidence specifying who removed what and when. Explaining every absence as successful suppression would make the proposition immune to correction. [34][U3]
Greek constructions of Zoroaster supply a stronger case of borrowed authority
Roger Beck documents Greek representations of Zoroaster and pseudepigraphic writings attributed to him. Such texts are genuine evidence that an authoritative foreign sage could be reconstructed for new audiences. Greek interpretations of the name involving stars belong to that reception history; they are not proof of the name’s original Iranian derivation. [36]
This supports a bounded version of the Patch’s concern with manufactured provenance. A work attributed to a prestigious ancient teacher can authorize later ideas. It does not identify a coordinated Chaldean operation, establish the alleged multilingual production program, or demonstrate that the historical Zoroaster taught the attributed doctrine. The source can be pseudepigraphic without being the product of the particular conspiracy proposed. [36][U3]
Temple property does not by itself establish the proposed banking continuity
Boyce’s account of fire cults documents endowments, land, patronage, and hereditary interests, drawing on legal and epigraphic evidence. This supports the economic importance of religious institutions. The further Patch claims about Jerusalem’s money changers migrating into Sasanian fire temples, mandatory currency conversion, and an equivalent set of exchange tables in 550 CE are not established by that evidence. Each would require contracts, named actors, regulations, or contemporary descriptions. [37][U3]
Several compressed origin stories need separate proofs
The Pharisee/Persian derivation should be removed as a settled premise. Schiffman also places the clearly named Pharisees in the Hasmonaean setting and stresses uncertainty about earlier origins. This prevents a simple lexical shortcut from the exile to later interpretive institutions. The Patch’s assertions of an exclusively Persian origin for afterlife doctrines and a single Babylonian origin for angelology similarly need doctrine-by-doctrine demonstrations; they cannot be established by the name of a group. [30][U3]
The description of a rebellion by overworked junior gods should be distinguished from Enūma Eliš’s Marduk–Tiamat conflict. That labor-rebellion opening belongs to Atrahasis; the texts can be compared, but their plots should not be merged into one primary witness. Ramantswana’s comparative discussion explicitly distinguishes them. A political reading becomes stronger when it preserves which text does what. [38]
The document’s claims about the synagogue’s precise exilic invention and pre-exilic “Sadducee-style” organization are not independently established in this investigation. They should remain hypotheses or analogies pending dated evidence. Likewise, recurrent 12/24/360 patterns alone cannot prove one exclusive inventor, one purpose, or one transmitting institution. The calendrical evidence above supplies an actual test case and a real complication. [U3][21][23]
The strongest defensible synthesis
Your three documents can be connected through a historically testable proposition: specialized literate communities could control access to technical and ritual knowledge, attach that knowledge to prestigious ancestors or divine revelation, and use its interpretation within economic and political institutions. Knowledge could then cross cultural boundaries and be recast under different authorities. The evidence supports particular instances and mechanisms; it does not presently support a single covert organization behind them all.
The Eethnic shield contributes the criterion for evaluating those mechanisms: when an interpretation fails, does the institution revise the interpretation and change its conduct, or preserve its authority by changing what counts as admissible evidence? Babylonia time supplies better historical anchors than the Patch’s largest origin claims, particularly through contracts, service rosters, and explicit astronomical comparisons. [U1][U2][U3]
Harland’s treatment of Hellenistic claims of civilizational priority strengthens the connection. Traditions that assign astral learning to Enoch or Abraham compete over the origins and legitimate ownership of wisdom. The opposition between authorized revelation and illicit Watcher instruction belongs to that contest. Such narratives disclose how authority was claimed; they do not certify the legendary attribution as historical fact. [24]
Hillman’s most productive contribution is therefore a set of questions for classical texts: which substances and ritual acts are actually named, which translations obscure their range, and when does a source authorize or condemn them? The comparison becomes weaker when a genuine drug reference is used to supply all the missing steps between pharmacology, priesthood, calendar administration, and modern power.
Your distinction between Babylon as an institutional function and Babylon as an ethnic population should remain explicit. A model of recurring institutional behavior can be compared across settings without assigning inherited culpability or presuming biological continuity. Modern political, abuse-network, and archaic-admixture claims in the source discussions are outside the evidence established by this investigation. [U2][U3]
