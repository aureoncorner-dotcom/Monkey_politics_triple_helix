Sacred Time and the Authority to Interpret
Epiphanius Ammon Hillman Timekeeper and the eethnic shield
A working essay for Andrew Dube  •  14 October 2026
A calendar tells a community when to act. A ritual tells a person how to pass from one condition to another. An interpreter explains why the appointed time and the prescribed action matter. Put these together and knowledge becomes an organizing power: it can coordinate work, distribute income, authorize worship, and define the route to salvation. The documents gathered here let us watch that process at several scales, from a Babylonian temple roster to a soul speaking at the gates of heaven.
Timekeeper is the project’s name for the ordering of cycles and passage. The eethnic shield names the further problem that arises when the authority explaining that order becomes protected from correction. Read together, they ask two connected questions: who controls the conditions of passage, and what happens when someone challenges that control? These are the project’s analytic terms. Their value emerges through the particular calendars, stories, words, and institutional arrangements they help us understand. [U1][U2]
Ammon Hillman’s discussions drew attention to the bodily language of ancient religion. Following that language into Epiphanius’s Panarion opens a much larger argument about where life belongs, how it circulates, and who has the right to explain it. Epiphanius, a fourth-century bishop of Salamis, preserves rival teachings inside a sustained attack on them. The resulting text lets us study both a cosmology of captivity and release and the authority that frames its surviving fragments. The Greek below is paired with this essay’s translations; the source guide identifies editions, other ancient witnesses, and the research behind the comparison.

When keeping time becomes organizing life
The clearest starting point is an ordinary institutional task: deciding whose turn it is. Babylonian temples coordinated priestly service through calendars, and those service rights carried income. Caroline Waerzeggers’s study shows how the organization of time could distribute both work and resources. BM 77834 reserves a thirteenth slot for an additional Addaru before the need for that month was known. A calendrical adjustment therefore had an administrative destination already waiting for it. [21]
Her discussion of Nabonidus’s reforms in 546 BCE makes the political dimension concrete. New oilpresser priesthoods and service shifts at Ebabbar belonged to a tightening of royal control. The sequence is intelligible: a calendar defines a service period; a service period assigns a right or duty; changing that arrangement changes who serves and who benefits. This is the Timekeeper connection at its most tangible. Timing becomes obligation through a rule, an office, and an allocation. [21]
The project archive expresses the question precisely: “who defines the calendar, who sets the ritual window, who interprets time, who turns timing into obligation?” The Babylonian Priesthood Function develops this as custody of time, texts, authorized interpretation, and legitimacy. Those questions invite close attention to the expertise itself. Francesca Rochberg describes sophisticated traditions of observation and learned interpretation; Kathryn Stevens examines protected knowledge and professional identity in late Babylonian Uruk. Intellectual achievement and the social organization of access belong in the same picture. [D2][D4][18][19]

That combination of continuity and revision matters for the eethnic shield. A calendar can be adjusted in response to observation, and an institution can decide how the consequences of an adjustment are shared. To understand its authority, we follow both processes: what corrects the rule, and who controls the correction? The temple roster and the astronomical scheme give these questions observable forms.
How knowledge travels and gains an ancestral voice
People and documents provide another scale of explanation. At Sippar, Kaššāya’s marriage agreements, BM 68921 and BM 65149, place a Judean family within Babylonian mercantile society. Tero Alstola identifies the groom’s family as mercantile and one witness, Bān-zēri, as a holder of an Ebabbar baker’s prebend. The family shared acquaintances with Marduk-rēmanni; the possible identification of the groom with a Guzānu in his archive remains uncertain. These are specific social connections through which exchange becomes historically imaginable. [20]
The Sippar–Ebabbar path ledger follows the same method. Its P007 route runs from Kaššāya through Šūzubu to Marduk-rēmanni. A path through named people helps us ask what moved along it: goods, obligations, documents, professional relationships, or instruction. The Borsippa sēpiru and BM 25563 lead belongs to the next stage of that work, where the cited document must identify what the alphabetic scribe actually did. The ledger’s strength is its ability to turn a large account of cultural contact into questions attached to records. [D3][U2]
A learned tradition can also travel under an ancestral name. Philip Harland’s study of civilizational priority explains how Babylonians, Egyptians, and Judeans competed over the antiquity and rightful ownership of wisdom. Accounts assigning astral learning to Enoch or Abraham make a claim about who may speak for it. The contrast between authorized revelation and illicit Watcher instruction adds a moral distinction: similar domains of knowledge can receive sharply different judgments according to their source and transmission. [24]
Greek and Roman accounts expose this work of interpretation. Diodorus describes Chaldean specialists through Greek categories; Jones and Steele compare his account with cuneiform evidence to identify both correspondences and reshaping. Pliny’s Natural History brings medicine, religion, and astrology together in its explanation of magic, with authorities ranging from Zoroaster to Moses and Greek philosophers. Roger Beck’s work on Greek representations of Zoroaster likewise shows how the name of an ancient foreign sage could authorize teachings for a new audience. Each case makes the interpreter part of the history of the knowledge being described. [12][13][36]
PGM XIII gives this conjunction a physical location. The Würzburg catalogue identifies the fourth-century Greek codex as P.Leid. I 395, now Leiden J 395. It contains versions of the Eighth Book of Moses alongside initiation, divination, and other ritual instructions. Matthew Andrews’s introduction describes a ritual seeking encounter with a supreme god and relief from astral fate, including ingestion of images of the astrological hours. An ancestral authority, embodied action, and cosmic timing meet inside one manuscript tradition. Here the Timekeeper connection concerns a ritual response to fate: the practitioner seeks a changed relationship to the powers that order life. [14][15]
The Apocryphon of John gives this problem a further textual foothold. Its long recension refers readers to a Book of Zoroaster after the account of bodily makers and passions (NHC II 19.6–10). The title connects its authorization of knowledge to the attributed wisdom discussed by Beck. Whether it names an actual source used by the redactor remains disputed: Waldstein and Wisse argue for borrowing, while Hugo Lundhaug considers a rhetorical or fictional reference possible. Porphyry independently records revelations circulating under Zoroaster’s and Zostrianus’s names, without identifying this particular book. The bridge is an attested strategy of attribution; a specific chain of borrowing still needs demonstration. [S3][S8][36]
The eethnic shield becomes relevant when we ask how such authority is kept answerable. Attribution to Moses or Zoroaster tells us how a work presents its legitimacy. Reading the actual instructions tells us what that legitimacy is being used to authorize. Keeping both in view allows us to appreciate the text’s ambitions while still asking fresh questions of it.
Hillman and the work of reading bodily language
Hillman brings a real classical training to this field. His 2004 Wisconsin–Madison dissertation, Representations of pharmacy in Roman literature from Cato to Ovid, and his 2001 article on the salamander as a drug in Nicander locate his early work in ancient pharmacology and literary interpretation. The Chemical Muse broadens the argument to drugs and classical culture; Original Sin and Hermaphrodites Gynomorphs and Jesus extend his public project toward religious origins. The catalogue records, publication details, and access notes are retained in the source guide. [1][2][3][4][6][7]
His most useful invitation is to take ancient bodily vocabulary seriously. In Odyssey 4, Helen puts a φάρμακον, pharmakon, into wine to relieve grief. The story associates her knowledge with Egypt. The Hymn to Demeter gives a different kind of specificity: water, barley meal, and pennyroyal are named ingredients in the drink. At the Judahite shrine of Arad, the published residue study reports cannabis on one altar and frankincense on another. Literary action, a named recipe, and material residue answer different questions, and together they show how productive a concrete inquiry into substances can be. [8][9][16][25]
A Greek word becomes informative through its sentence. φάρμακον can concern a drug, remedy, or poison; Plato uses it in the Phaedrus discussion of writing and memory. φαρμακεία has a range including drug-related and magical practice. Revelation 18:23 uses its dative form, φαρμακείᾳ, in the accusation that Babylon deceives the nations. χριστός carries the sense of anointed: the Greek of Isaiah 45:1 applies it to Cyrus. Context tells us which part of a word’s range is active and what an author is doing with it. [11][17][25][26][27]
This is also how to evaluate a provocative reconstruction fairly. Philippe Charlier’s BMCR review of The Chemical Muse objects to steps from literary drug scenes to claims about authors’ or audiences’ consumption. The useful question in that dispute is what joins one step to the next. A recipe, a residue, a fictional scene, and a hostile accusation each offer evidence of a particular kind. The reader can build a strong account by making those joins visible. In the Panarion, the crucial first step is to hear when Epiphanius narrates, when he reports a rival voice, and when he supplies his own interpretation. [5]
Gathering the soul from the world
The central verb in Epiphanius’s account is “gather.” The teachings he attributes to his rivals imagine soul or divine power dispersed through living things. Eating can therefore be understood as collecting that power and carrying it upward. Semen and menstrual blood enter the explanation as bearers of life. The controversial bodily acts acquire a purpose within a theory of recovery.
26.9.4   Epiphanius reports the rivals’ explanation
τὴν δὲ δύναμιν τὴν ἐν τοῖς καταμηνίοις καὶ ἐν ταῖς γοναῖς ψυχὴν εἶναί φασιν, ἣν συλλέγοντες ἐσθίομεν
“They say that the power in menstrual discharges and in the generative emissions is soul, which we gather and eat.”
The voice changes inside the sentence. φασιν means “they say”; ἐσθίομεν means “we eat.” Epiphanius first attributes the teaching and then gives its speakers a first-person voice. The “we” is the community as he represents it. In the continuation, the explanation extends to meat, vegetables, and bread: the gathered soul is taken into the heavens. The movement is from dispersed life to collected life, then from the lower world toward its source.
That logic illuminates the story he attributes to the Greater Questions of Mary. A Jesus figure takes Mary up a mountain, produces a woman from his side, has intercourse with that woman, and partakes of his emission. Mary witnesses the action and becomes alarmed. The grammatical unit is unusually important because it identifies who performs the final act.
26.8.2   The crucial grammatical unit
τὴν ἀπόρροιαν αὐτοῦ μεταλαβόντα
“having partaken of his emission”
μεταλαβόντα is masculine accusative and belongs to the sequence describing the Jesus figure. The bodily reading has grammatical support within this reported story. The surrounding action supplies the sexual sense of “emission,” while the verb expresses partaking. At 26.8.7, a communal “we ourselves eat it” appears within a reading of Psalm 1. A revelation narrative, a practice attributed to a community, and a scriptural explanation are being made to illuminate one another. The unit we are reading is that narrated theological argument. [S1]
Following the purpose of the actions changes the inquiry. The question becomes how a human being might recover a self thought to be scattered through the world. Reproduction, eating, and salvation now occupy the same conceptual field. The next passages explain why gathering matters: the soul must confront rulers who can keep it within the cycle of embodied life.

In the 365-name rite, a ruler is invoked at each sexual act. A traversal and a return traversal produce the total of 730. Epiphanius interrupts the claimed ascent with “or rather, descends,” turning the practitioners’ spiritual language into his own moral judgment. His arithmetic counts acts; the calendrical resonance comes into focus when other ancient accounts are placed beside it.

26.9.9   The practitioner’s culminating declaration
ἐγώ εἰμι ὁ Χριστός, ἐπειδὴ ἄνωθεν καταβέβηκα διὰ τῶν ὀνομάτων τῶν τξεʹ ἀρχόντων
“I am Christ, because I have descended from above through the names of the 365 rulers.”
The “I” is the practitioner at the culmination of the sequence. Speaking the names and completing the passage enable a claim to an origin above the powers. The eight-heaven list and the 365-name cycle thus contribute different aspects of the same problem: where authority is located and how someone moves through it. Epiphanius leaves their detailed coordination unexplained. We can still follow the theological work each performs.
Irenaeus makes the calendrical association explicit in his report on Basilides: the 365 heavens correspond to the year’s days (1.24.3), while knowledge of the powers enables passage (1.24.5–6). That gives the Timekeeper comparison a textual anchor. The comparison below distinguishes this claim from other uses of 365 and from the differently timed route in Pistis Sophia. [S2][S5]
The Timekeeper comparison becomes precise here. In the temple calendar, an ordered cycle determines when a person serves. In the cosmology, an ordered system determines how a soul moves, waits, or returns. Both arrangements make passage depend on conditions that specialists explain. The historical settings differ, while the relation between order, knowledge, and permission can be compared directly.

The prospect of escape gives this comparison its force. In Epiphanius’s report, the ignorant soul is swallowed by a dragonlike ruler and returned to embodiment. A person who has gathered the soul can pass beyond the rulers and reach Barbelo, the Mother of the living. At 26.10.9, the gathered person is “no longer held here.” Release means a changed relation to the system: the power that once detained the person can be passed. [S1]
26.13.2   The ascent formula attributed to a Gospel of Philip
ἐπέγνων ἐμαυτήν, φησί, καὶ συνέλεξα ἐμαυτὴν ἐκ πανταχόθεν, καὶ οὐκ ἔσπειρα τέκνα τῷ ἄρχοντι, ἀλλὰ ἐξερρίζωσα τὰς ῥίζας αὐτοῦ καὶ συνέλεξα τὰ μέλη τὰ διεσκορπισμένα, καὶ οἶδά σε τίς εἶ. ἐγὼ γάρ, φησί, τῶν ἄνωθέν εἰμι.
“I have recognized myself,” it says, “and gathered myself from every direction. I have not sown children for the ruler, but have uprooted his roots and gathered the scattered members. I know who you are. For I,” it says, “am one of those from above.”
The ascent formula is attributed to a Gospel of Philip transmitted in Epiphanius’s account. ψυχή, “soul,” is grammatically feminine, which explains the feminine self-reference. The speech links knowing oneself, gathering the scattered members, recognizing the ruler, and refusing to sow children for him. The short expression συνέλεξα ἐμαυτήν, “I gathered myself,” states the purpose. Reproduction extends the self’s attachment to the lower world; recovery makes departure possible. At 26.13.3, a soul with a child is detained until its children can be recovered.
The Elijah story turns that principle into a confrontation. A female demon blocks his ascent and claims children conceived from his nocturnal emissions. Elijah insists that he lived chastely, but the story makes dispersed substance a problem even where conscious intention was chaste.
26.13.5   The demon’s objection
οὐ δύνασαι ἀνελθεῖν καὶ ὧδε ἀφεῖναι τὰ τέκνα σου
“You cannot ascend and leave your children here.”
Epiphanius answers with a body-and-spirit objection: how could a bodiless spirit receive bodily substance and become pregnant? He also contests the appeal to Jude’s “dreamers,” interpreting it as confused thought. His reply and the rival story therefore disagree about the relation between material life and spiritual responsibility. The Timekeeper reading follows the story’s mechanism: something left below supplies a claim that can hold the traveler back. [S1]

The numerical comparison needs particular care. In the long Apocryphon of John, NHC II 11.22–26 gives a sequence of rulers, seven powers, and six angels for each power, then states a total of 365. The transmitted scheme does not yield a straightforward calculation of 7 × 6 = 365. Its wording and arrangement also vary across recensions. Likewise, John’s three names for one chief ruler must not be imposed on Epiphanius’s separate heavenly placements. Shared numbers and names invite comparison; their functions must be established within each account. [S1][S3]
Pistis Sophia makes the timing mechanism especially explicit. The righteous but uninitiated soul receives instruction, is judged by the Virgin of Light, and waits with little Sabaoth until Zeus and Aphrodite stand before her and Kronos and Ares behind her. Purification is followed by two different drinks: Yaluham’s water erases previous memories; another receiver then gives wisdom and soberness before renewed embodiment, producing persistent pursuit of the mysteries. This is a sequence of forgetting and reorientation, rather than uninterrupted recollection. [S5]
These comparisons strengthen the Timekeeper question by showing different conditions of passage: counted names, recovered power, changed allegiance, instruction, and a timed return. They also extend the eethnic shield question to every witness. Revelation can challenge an established interpreter while supplying a new claim to authority. Its narrative form alone does not establish that it is immune to correction. The test remains how a claim meets questions, competing readings, and contrary evidence. Irenaeus’s earlier gathering account makes the cosmological comparison historically deeper, but neither his report nor the independent Coptic works confirms Epiphanius’s particular rites or identifies all these writers with one community.
The surviving Nag Hammadi Gospel of Philip develops its own account of resurrection and transformation during life. It is a separate textual witness from the ascent speech Epiphanius quotes under that title. Keeping both works visible enriches the comparison: ancient writers could ask related questions about transformation while giving different answers about its means. The shared problem is how embodied life relates to a higher origin; the answers remain available for close reading in their own texts. [S6]
Who may reveal what a sanctuary conceals
The same conflict over interpretation appears in the Birth of Mary story reported by Epiphanius. Zechariah enters the sanctuary, sees a figure in the form of an ass, and seeks to expose what he has discovered. His mouth is stopped. When he later speaks, the story says, he is killed. The revelation is condensed into a question.
26.12.3   The interrupted revelation
οὐαὶ ὑμῖν, τίνι προσκυνεῖτε;
“Woe to you! Whom are you worshipping?”
Priestly bells receive a striking explanation: their sound warns the hidden object of worship that a priest is approaching, giving it time to conceal itself. Epiphanius records the purpose with κρύπτηται, “so that he may hide.” Within the rival story, a ritual safeguard protects a deception. The eethnic shield connection is the organization of access to truth: the person who sees behind the authorized account becomes a threat to the institution the account supports.
Epiphanius replies by testing the story’s account of embodiment and its biblical chronology. Would an invisible spirit need an audible warning to avoid being seen? Luke’s narrative also has Zechariah living after John’s birth and speaking prophecy. The reader can follow those objections as arguments, comparing the rival story with the physical assumptions and scriptural narrative Epiphanius invokes. [S1][S7]
His defense of Sabaoth addresses the authority of naming. The rival reading turns the designation into a lower ruler’s name. Epiphanius interprets “Lord Sabaoth” as praise of God, appealing to hosts or armies: Hebrew ṣəḇāʾōt means “hosts,” within the fuller title “Lord of hosts.”
26.10.11   Epiphanius’s interpretation of the title
οὐκ ὄνομά τινος εἶπεν, ἀλλ᾽ ὄνομα δοξολογίας τῆς θεότητος
“It did not give someone’s name, but a designation praising the divinity.”
The disagreement is about what a sacred name points to. A title that authorizes worship in one account identifies an obstacle in another. The sanctuary story asks who is being worshipped; the discussion of Sabaoth asks how language tells the worshipper whom to recognize. Both connect the interpretation of a sign to the legitimacy of an institution. [S1][D1]
How scripture defines the body and the community
Epiphanius often contests rival readings with words both sides recognize. At 26.8.7, he reports an interpretation of Psalm 1 in which the tree’s fruit signifies emission and its unfailing leaf means that the substance is consumed rather than dropped. At 26.15.1, he restores the preceding verse and asks the reader to hear the image within the psalm’s account of the righteous person.
26.15.1   The context Epiphanius restores
ἐν τῷ νόμῳ κυρίου τὸ θέλημα αὐτοῦ
“His delight is in the law of the Lord.”
The blessed person delights in the Lord’s law and meditates on it day and night. That context gives Epiphanius a substantive objection to readers who reject the lawgiver while claiming the flourishing tree as their model. This is a useful example of correction through surrounding language: the interpretation can be tested against the passage it cites.
His treatment of Mary and the risen Jesus shows a different interpretive move. He quotes John 20:17 within an argument about chastity.
26.15.6   John 20.17 within Epiphanius’s argument
μή μου ἅπτου· οὔπω γὰρ ἀναβέβηκα πρὸς τὸν πατέρα
“Do not touch me,” or “Do not hold on to me,” “for I have not yet ascended to the Father.”
ἅπτομαι can mean touching or taking hold and can carry a sexual sense in a suitable setting, as in 1 Corinthians 7:1. John’s scene is the encounter with Mary Magdalene after the resurrection and continues with a message for the disciples. Epiphanius introduces his explanation with ἵνα δείξῃ, “so that he might show,” assigning the saying a purpose: to display chastity unmixed with bodies. The grammar lets us see the interpreter at work. John supplies the saying; Epiphanius supplies this account of what it is intended to demonstrate. [S1][S7]
His defense of Christ’s flesh likewise gathers Johannine warnings into a doctrinal boundary and applies it to his opponents. In the passages on marriage, the positive teaching becomes clearer. Epiphanius honors both celibacy and marriage: abstinence can be chaste, and a faithful marriage can have an undefiled bed.
26.16.1   Hebrews 13.4 in his defense of marriage
τίμιος ὁ γάμος καὶ ἡ κοίτη ἀμίαντος
“Marriage is honorable, and the bed undefiled.”
The conflict over “virginity” follows from these different accounts of bodily life. He says the rivals use the term for women who avoid insemination despite sexual activity, whereas he reserves it for abstinence. One definition seeks to prevent reproduction and the dispersal of life; the other defines a form of sexual conduct. Understanding that difference makes the argument’s stakes much clearer than its insults do.

Paul, Jude, and the pastoral letters provide Epiphanius with language for judging desire, conscience, household life, and rival authority. At 26.16.3, he combines difficult last days, love of pleasure, the prohibition of marriage, and seared consciences. κατ’ αὐτῶν, “against them,” and περὶ τούτων, “about these people,” show him assigning the warnings to the people he is attacking. The quotations establish his moral framework; his application identifies his opponents as its target. [S1][S7]
The eethnic shield asks what happens after that identification. If a sacred warning settles who the opponent is before their words are heard, interpretation can make its own verdict difficult to challenge. Yet a shared text can also supply a standard by which the interpreter is judged. The project’s use of Malachi 2:7–9 develops this constructive possibility: priestly knowledge carries obligations, and the office can be criticized for violating them. Authority becomes answerable through the responsibilities it claims. [U1][D1]
The eethnic shield and the decision to let a teaching be heard
Epiphanius makes his policy of disclosure explicit at 26.14.4. The passage moves from judging a teaching to controlling its circulation in speech, writing, and hearing.
26.14.4   The burial passage
καὶ ἔδει μὲν ἀληθῶς ταῦτα μήτε λέγεσθαι μήτε ἐν συντάγμασι καταξιοῦσθαι γράφεσθαι, θάπτεσθαι δὲ ὥσπερ νεκρόν τινα ὀδωδότα καὶ λοιμώδη ἀποφορὰν πνέοντα, ἵνα μὴ καὶ διὰ τῆς ἀκοῆς λυμήνηται τοὺς ἀνθρώπους.
“These things really ought neither to be spoken nor considered worthy of being written in treatises, but to be buried like a stinking corpse breathing a pestilential stench, so that they might not harm people even through hearing.”
He then explains why he writes about it anyway. If the teaching had disappeared, silence would be best; because he says it remains active, he recounts selected parts to warn hearers and turn people away. Disclosure is presented as a protective duty. That decision also shapes the archive available to later readers: the opponent selects the fragments and introduces the explanation under which they are encountered. [S1]
26.17.9   The expulsion
καὶ τοὺς αὐτοὺς ἀπελαθῆναι τῆς πόλεως, ὀνόματα περί που ὀγδοήκοντα
“And for those same people to be expelled from the city, about eighty persons [literally, ‘names’].”
Epiphanius ’s authority over this material does not rest on reading alone. At 26.17.4–9 he says he encountered adherents of the sect in his youth. Frank Williams places the episode during his Egyptian years, at Alexandria, although the passage itself identifies neither country nor city (Book I, introduction, p. xiii). Women belonging to the group approached him and tried to draw him in, and he read its books. He casts his escape as divine rescue through the biblical comparison with Joseph and Potiphar’s wife. He then reported the members to the bishops in that place, identifying those concealed within the church, and says that about eighty were expelled from the city. No numerical age is given. By his own account, the writer who decides how this teaching should be heard was also the informant whose report led to its adherents’ removal. [S1]
That changes how the burial passage can be read. Silencing a teaching and expelling the people who hold it apply a single judgment: the danger is contagion, and the remedy is removal. The eethnic shield asks whether an interpreter can be corrected. Here the interpreter presents himself as having acted on his interpretation before writing this account, so the treatise can also be read as a defense of a decision already carried out. His testimony deserves the same scrutiny he applies to his rivals. April DeConick likewise links his report against eighty people to her judgment that the story is “slanted and exaggerated to his own benefit” (“Is marriage salvation?”, 18 June 2010). This is her critical interpretation of his narrative, not independent confirmation of the episode. The episode gives him eyewitness credibility, a story of resisted temptation, and a successful outcome. It may preserve real events, but the surviving account of this particular episode is his own, written by the person whose standing it raises. Like the fragments he quotes, it records both his claims about what happened and how he wished it to be remembered. [S1]
Textual note. The Greek counts “about eighty names,” an idiom for persons. The names of the 365 rulers at 26.9.6–9 belong to a different context of invocation; the shared word permits comparison but does not establish an intentional echo. Greek checked in Gilski/Baron, Panarion: Herezje 1–33 (2015), printed pp. 284–286, especially p. 286 (PDF p. 144; Holl I, p. 298). [S1]
This is the closest point of contact with the eethnic shield’s concern about interpretive authority. The user’s phrase in the archive is direct: “they decide what’s real and what isn’t.” The surrounding discussion asks what happens when an institution determines whose testimony counts while treating its own credibility as settled. Epiphanius gives the question a literary form. He decides what the audience should hear and how it should understand the danger. At the same time, the words he preserves give future readers material with which to examine his decision. [D1]

The Hippocratic On the Sacred Disease supplies an especially clear account of an explanation that protects its custodian. Its author attacks purifiers who call an illness divine: recovery enhances their reputation, while failure is attributed to the gods. Either outcome can leave the practitioners’ authority intact. The eethnic shield helps us describe the mechanism and ask what would count as correction. Because the medical author is also advancing a rival explanation, the same question remains available for his own claims. [10]
Plato’s Phaedrus approaches the problem through writing. Theuth offers it as an aid to memory and wisdom; Thamus asks whether reminders produce understanding. A written statement cannot answer a fresh question as a knowledgeable speaker can. The story also associates Theuth with technical arts, including astronomy. It brings us back to the relation between a useful representation and the living work of interpreting it. A calendar, a treatise, or an authorized quotation becomes more useful when readers can question how it applies. [11]
The constructive lesson is a practice of correction. Place the preserved words beside the explanation. Follow their subjects and speakers. Read the surrounding sentences. Ask what response would change the interpreter’s account or conduct. That practice can be applied to Epiphanius, Hillman, the medical polemicist, and this essay. It keeps the project’s criticism connected to the possibility of learning.
Time empire and the changing life of an inherited idea
The Babylonian Patch extends the inquiry toward Iranian traditions and political theology. Here, too, the strongest teaching comes from following particular transformations. Avestan zruuan concerns time; an epithet such as akarana expresses boundlessness. The later Zurvan myth makes time part of a divine genealogy. Gherardo Gnoli interprets the development through contact with Babylonian astral culture, while Albert de Jong challenges proposed origins and the reconstruction of a distinct Zurvanite movement. These competing accounts direct attention to dated witnesses and stages of interpretation. [33][34][35][U3]
De Jong’s discussion of fifth-century Sasanian court theology provides a concrete political setting: Ełišē reports Mihr-Narseh’s letter urging Armenians to accept Zoroastrianism. Gnoli’s treatment distinguishes the twin spirits of Yasna 30.3–4 from the later genealogy in which Zurvan fathers Ohrmazd and Ahriman. The analytical task is to follow how a theological formulation acquires a new setting and purpose. De Jong also considers late Sasanian marginalization of Zurvanite formulations as a historical possibility. These are questions about change, authority, and the survival of competing explanations. [34][35]
Institutions give ideas an economic setting as well. Mary Boyce’s account of fire cults documents endowed foundations, land, patronage, and hereditary interests. Those arrangements make religious continuity material: rights and resources help sustain practice. Research into a proposed institutional succession then proceeds through contracts, offices, and named people, just as it does in the Sippar ledger. [37]
Language helps us keep the relevant roles visible. Old Persian magu and Greek μάγος have a real lexical relationship, while Greek usage expands beyond one Iranian priestly role. The standard account of “Pharisee” relates to separation or distinction: the Aramaic pryš family differs from prsy, “Persian.” Schiffman locates the clearly named Pharisees in the Hasmonaean setting and discusses the uncertainty of earlier origins; an older Persian derivation remains a disputed proposal. Reading words in their scripts and dated settings gives the project firmer historical questions than resemblance in English alone. [28][29][30][31][32]
The same care improves political readings of myth. Atrahasis opens with the labor rebellion of overworked junior gods; Enūma Eliš develops the Marduk–Tiamat conflict. Preserving those distinct plots lets us ask how each imagines work, rule, and the establishment of order. The project’s Set–Osiris architecture belongs to its own interpretive construction and can be developed as such. The comparative question concerns the work an account of order performs in its setting. [38][U2]
Across these materials, the recurring pattern becomes teachable. Specialists calculate, preserve, and interpret an order. Communities attach duties, hopes, and rights to that order. Some texts promise a privileged passage through it; others promise release from it. Authorities then judge those promises and decide how rival accounts may circulate. Timekeeper follows the organization of the cycle and the conditions of movement. The eethnic shield follows the conditions under which the organizer and interpreter can be corrected.
The evidence brings those connections into focus at different scales: an extra month in a service roster, a conversion between time schemes, a manuscript invoking Moses and astral fate, a soul gathering itself before the rulers, and a bishop explaining why a teaching should be buried. These examples support a history built from particular mechanisms and relationships. The continuing work is to connect them with the same precision: the person, passage, procedure, and institutional decision that make a proposed link concrete.

Babylon's pharmakeia: Isaiah, Nahum, and Revelation
Supplement added 15 October 2026 for Hillman Epiphanius Timekeeper and the eethnic shield.
This supplements the existing discussion, "Babylon and pharmakeia occur in the same biblical passage." Existing text and source numbering are retained. PH source identifiers below belong only to this supplement.

The Greek comparison

Isaiah 47:9, 12 (Septuagint) addresses Babylon. Its phrase ἐν τῇ φαρμακείᾳ σου means "in/with your sorcery." Verse 12 pairs it with ἐν ταῖς ἐπαοιδαῖς σου καὶ τῇ πολλῇ φαρμακείᾳ σου: "with your incantations and your abundant sorcery." These are our close translations of the Greek, not quotations from an English Bible. The paired incantations and the challenge to astrologers in verse 13 make the magical-divinatory setting explicit. The taunt is that these resources cannot prevent disaster. "Despite your sorcery" captures that contextual contrast; "despite" is not an automatic dictionary meaning of ἐν. The Septuagint is an ancient Greek translation of Isaiah, not Isaiah's original language. [PH01]

Nahum 3:4 (Septuagint) concerns Nineveh, as verse 7 confirms. It depicts the city as a prostitute and describes ἡ πωλοῦσα ἔθνη ἐν τῇ πορνείᾳ αὐτῆς καὶ φυλὰς ἐν τοῖς φαρμάκοις αὐτῆς: "she who sells nations through her prostitution, and peoples through her sorceries." The selling language belongs to the personified city's accusation; it does not identify a drug transaction. Nahum uses plural forms of φάρμακον, not the identical noun φαρμακεία. The connection is therefore related vocabulary plus a closely comparable image: a seductive imperial city and the nations it ensnares. [PH02]

Revelation 18:23 reads, in the Nestle 1904 text, ἐν τῇ φαρμακίᾳ σου ἐπλανήθησαν πάντα τὰ ἔθνη: "through your sorcery all the nations were led astray." Other displayed Greek editions spell the noun φαρμακείᾳ. That spelling difference does not by itself establish a different sense. Here ἐν with the dative naturally marks means, while the passive verb states the result: nations were deceived or led astray. The clause does not specify the physical procedure. The preceding statement concerns Babylon's powerful merchants, but juxtaposition does not grammatically define sorcery as commerce. [PH03]

What carries over, and what changes

Isaiah supplies Babylon's sorcery and the failure of her protective expertise. Nahum supplies an especially close combination of prostitution, sorceries, and nations under a city's power. Revelation brings comparable prophetic images into its own condemnation of Babylon. These are observable textual parallels supporting an intertextual reading; they are not a demonstrated historical chain connecting the same practitioners across the three books. Nahum also prevents us from claiming that international seduction first entered this imagery in Revelation. [PH01–PH03]

The commercial and political interpretation rests on the larger literary setting. Revelation 18:3 joins the nations' intoxication, the kings' prostitution, and merchants enriched by Babylon's luxury. Reading 18:23 as an image of corrupting imperial influence therefore has contextual support. Wiriya Tipvarakankoon's study reviews literal and figurative interpretations and relates Babylon's deception to the chapter's economic critique. That is an interpretive argument, not an alternative dictionary definition of the Greek noun. [PH04, PH07]

What the Greek supports

The accusation is expressed in magical language. LSJ documents medical, poisonous, and magical uses of the word family; a passage must determine which sense is appropriate. A word's connection to drugs does not establish that this passage describes a pharmacological operation. Conversely, interpreting the city figuratively does not remove the magical associations of the chosen word. Literal ritual associations and figurative political force can coexist. [PH05, PH07]

The most defensible formulation is: Revelation portrays Babylon as using sorcery to lead the nations astray; its commercial and political setting supports a figurative application, while the verse alone does not settle whether particular magical practices are also intended. "Deceived" does not mean that the author specifies counterfeit stage magic. Bratcher and Hatton's translation handbook cautions against inserting "false" before "magic." [PH03, PH06, PH07]

Connection to the existing research

This comparison extends the document's existing lexical intersection with Hillman's field. It does not verify an additional claim by Hillman without an exact attributable passage and supporting evidence. Babylonia time and THE BABYLONIAN PRIESTHOOD-FUNCTION supply questions about expertise and authority; the eethnic shield supplies a framework for examining how interpretations are defended. Those project connections remain distinct from the primary Greek evidence.

Isaiah's astrologers make celestial expertise a relevant comparative topic. These verses do not establish calendar administration, a named priestly office, a specific substance or dose, or organizational continuity. The Timekeeper distinction between research and symbolism, and Cosmic Time Architecture III's separation of geometry, mechanism, history, and symbolism, remain applicable. No mathematical result or historical transmission claim is upgraded by this addition.
